A permanent engineer is usually the better deal. They accumulate context, they care about the roadmap in a way a contractor structurally cannot, and over three years they are cheaper.

That is the honest starting position, and any firm selling embedded engineers that will not say it out loud is selling rather than advising.

There are, though, specific situations where embedding wins clearly.

## When embedding is genuinely better

**The role has been open for a quarter.** Hiring under time pressure is how companies hire badly. An embedded engineer removes the pressure, which usually improves the permanent hire you eventually make.

**The need has an end date.** A three-month push, a migration, a compliance deadline. Hiring permanently for temporary work leaves you with a difficult conversation in month five.

**You need seniority you cannot yet justify full-time.** Architecture decisions, review standards, technical direction. Real value, but not forty hours a week of it. A few days a month of senior input is a shape the permanent market does not offer.

**One person is the bottleneck.** Everything routes through them for review. Another permanent hire adds to their review queue before it helps. A senior second pair of eyes reduces it immediately.

**You need capability transferred, not just work done.** A team that wants to own a new area and needs someone alongside them while they learn it.

## When you should just hire

**The work is permanent and core.** If this is your product and the need is indefinite, hire. Anyone who tells you otherwise is optimising for their own revenue.

**You need deep domain knowledge.** Some businesses take a year to understand. That is a permanent-hire investment.

**Budget is genuinely tight.** Embedded capacity costs more per month than salary. It buys speed and flexibility, not savings.

## The questions that actually matter

If you do go this route, these separate a partner from a body shop.

**Do we interview the specific people?** You should meet them, by name, before anything starts. "We'll assign someone from the team" means a pool, and a pool means rotation.

**Whose process do they work in?** Yours. Your repository, your board, your ceremonies, your definition of done. Someone importing a different process is adding friction and calling it methodology.

**What happens if it is not working?** Replacing an engineer who is not a fit should be the firm's problem and the firm's cost, not a new search for you. That is the main thing separating this from staffing.

**Can we hire them permanently?** The answer should be yes with no conversion fee. If a firm blocks this, they are prioritising their revenue over your outcome, and that tells you how the rest of the relationship will go.

**What is left when you leave?** Documentation and pairing should be inside the engagement. If your team is no stronger afterwards, you rented output and learned nothing.

## The honest economics

A permanent senior engineer in the US costs meaningfully more than salary once you include benefits, equipment, recruitment and the ramp-up period before they are productive. Embedded capacity costs more per month and starts producing in days rather than months, with no severance risk at the end.

Under about six months, embedding usually wins on total cost. Beyond a year, hiring wins. In between it depends on how confident you are that the need persists.

That is roughly the whole calculation, and it is not complicated. Be suspicious of anyone who makes it sound like it is.
